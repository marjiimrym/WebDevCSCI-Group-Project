The Sun

Star

The star at the centre of our solar system. It holds 99.8 percent of all the mass in it.

Overview

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star (G2V) at the center of our Solar System. It contains 99.86% of the system's mass and its gravity holds everything in orbit. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old and roughly halfway through its main-sequence lifetime. It converts hydrogen into helium through nuclear fusion in its core, releasing enormous energy.

Every second, the Sun fuses about 600 million tons of hydrogen, releasing energy equivalent to billions of nuclear weapons. This energy radiates outward, taking thousands of years to reach the surface, then 8 minutes to reach Earth.

Structure & Layers

The Sun's interior has distinct layers: the core (fusion zone, ~25% of radius), the radiative zone (energy moves by radiation), and the convective zone (energy moves by rising hot plasma). The visible surface is the photosphere (where light escapes).

Above the photosphere lie the chromosphere (reddish, seen during eclipses) and the corona (million-degree outer atmosphere, source of solar wind). The corona's extreme heat remains a scientific mystery — magnetic fields likely play a key role.

Solar Activity

The Sun follows an ~11-year solar cycle of magnetic activity. At solar maximum: numerous sunspots (cooler magnetic regions), solar flares (sudden energy releases), and coronal mass ejections (CMEs — massive plasma eruptions). These events drive space weather, affecting satellites, power grids, and creating auroras.

The solar wind — a constant stream of charged particles — shapes planetary magnetospheres and blows comet tails. Earth's magnetic field protects us from most effects.

Future Evolution

In ~5 billion years, the Sun will exhaust its core hydrogen and expand into a red giant, potentially engulfing Mercury, Venus, and possibly Earth. It will then shed its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf — a dense, Earth-sized remnant that will cool over trillions of years.
