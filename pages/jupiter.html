Jupiter

Planet

The largest planet, a banded gas giant with a storm wider than Earth that has raged for centuries.

Overview

Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun and the largest in the Solar System — more than twice as massive as all other planets combined. A gas giant composed mostly of hydrogen and helium, it lacks a solid surface. Its rapid rotation (9.9 hours) creates powerful banded cloud patterns and the most intense magnetic field of any planet.

Jupiter acts as a "cosmic vacuum cleaner," its gravity deflecting or capturing comets and asteroids that might threaten the inner planets. It has a faint ring system and at least 95 known moons, including the four large Galilean moons.

Atmosphere & Great Red Spot

Jupiter's visible bands are ammonia clouds in different altitudes and temperatures. The Great Red Spot is an anticyclonic storm at least 350 years old, large enough to swallow Earth. It rotates counterclockwise every 6 days and has been shrinking over recent decades. Smaller storms appear and merge constantly.

Deep below the clouds, pressure and temperature rise dramatically. At ~20% of the radius, hydrogen becomes metallic — a fluid conductor generating the immense magnetic field. The core may be a dense mix of rock and metal under millions of atmospheres.

Galilean Moons

Jupiter's four largest moons (discovered by Galileo in 1610) are worlds in their own right:

  • Io — Most volcanically active body in Solar System, sulfur-covered, tidally heated
  • Europa — Smooth ice crust over a global subsurface ocean, prime candidate for life
  • Ganymede — Largest moon in Solar System (bigger than Mercury), has own magnetic field
  • Callisto — Heavily cratered, ancient surface, likely subsurface ocean

All four are locked in orbital resonances (Io:Europa:Ganymede = 1:2:4) that drive tidal heating.

Exploration

Pioneer 10/11 (1970s), Voyager 1/2 (1979), Galileo (1995-2003), and Juno (2016-present) have studied Jupiter. Juno's polar orbits reveal chaotic cyclones at the poles, a "fuzzy" core, and deep atmospheric dynamics. Europa Clipper (launch 2024) and JUICE (ESA, en route) will explore the icy moons in the 2030s.
