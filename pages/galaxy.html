Overview

The Andromeda Galaxy (Messier 31, M31, or NGC 224) is a barred spiral galaxy approximately 2.5 million light-years from Earth. It is the nearest major galaxy to the Milky Way and the largest galaxy in the Local Group, containing roughly one trillion stars — several times more than our galaxy. Visible to the naked eye under dark skies, it appears as a faint, elongated smudge spanning about 3° of sky (six full Moons wide).

Andromeda and the Milky Way are gravitationally bound and approaching each other at ~110 km/s. In ~4-5 billion years, they will begin a merger that will transform both into a single elliptical galaxy ("Milkdromeda").

Structure

Andromeda is a classic barred spiral with a central bar (~27,000 ly long), a bright bulge of old stars, and prominent spiral arms traced by young blue stars, H II regions, and dust lanes. The disk is warped at the edges, likely from interactions with satellite galaxies. A massive dark matter halo extends far beyond the visible disk.

The core hosts a supermassive black hole (~140 million solar masses) with a peculiar double nucleus — possibly two merging black holes or a single black hole with an eccentric stellar disk. The bulge contains mostly old, metal-rich stars; the disk hosts ongoing star formation.

Satellite Galaxies

Andromeda has a rich system of dwarf satellite galaxies (over 30 confirmed), including the bright ellipticals M32 (compact, likely stripped core) and M110 (dwarf elliptical with dust). Many satellites lie in a vast, rotating plane — a structure challenging standard cosmological models. Streams of tidally disrupted dwarfs (like the Giant Stellar Stream) wrap around Andromeda, evidence of ongoing cannibalism.

Future Collision

Andromeda and the Milky Way are on a collision course. Current data suggests a first close pass in ~4 billion years, triggering bursts of star formation as gas clouds compress. The galaxies will swing past each other, then fall back and fully merge in ~6-7 billion years. The Sun will likely be flung to a new orbit; direct stellar collisions are extremely rare due to vast distances.

The resulting "Milkdromeda" will be a giant elliptical galaxy with little gas for new stars. By then, the Sun will be a red giant or white dwarf, and Earth uninhabitable. The Local Group's other galaxies (Triangulum, Magellanic Clouds, dwarfs) will eventually join the merger.

Observing Andromeda

Best seen in autumn (Northern Hemisphere), locate it by finding the Great Square of Pegasus and star-hopping from Alpheratz. Binoculars reveal the bright core and oval glow; telescopes (6"+ aperture) show dust lanes and companions M32/M110. Astrophotography captures its full spiral grandeur. Edwin Hubble's 1923-24 observations of Cepheid variables in Andromeda proved it lay far beyond the Milky Way, settling the "Great Debate" and establishing the extragalactic distance scale.
